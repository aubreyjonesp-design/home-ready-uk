How much water to store for an emergency (UK)

Last checked: 18 October 2026

Water is the thing most UK homes are missing in an emergency. Here is what the official advice says, worked out for your household, and what to do if the taps stop.

What the official advice says

  • GOV.UK Prepare says there is no standard figure, because emergencies last different lengths of time and people use different amounts.
  • It quotes the World Health Organization: at least 2.5 to 3 litres of drinking water per person per day for survival.
  • 10 litres per person per day is more comfortable, because it also covers basic cooking and washing.
  • You may need more for making up baby formula, for medical devices and for pets.
  • GOV.UK recommends buying sealed bottled water rather than filling your own containers from the tap, because it is sealed in a food-grade bottle and keeps better.

How much for 3 days

We worked these out from the GOV.UK figures above (3 litres and 10 litres per person per day). They are a guide, not an official table.

For example, a 6-pack of 2-litre bottles holds 12 litres. Our kit and water calculator works it out for your household, including babies and pets.

Storing it without waste

  • Buy a pack or two at a time, rather than all at once.
  • Follow the storage instructions and the date on the bottle, and use the oldest first, replacing it as you go.
  • Keep it somewhere you can reach easily, and spread it out if you are short of space.

If your water goes off

  • Check your water company’s website for updates and where to collect water (GOV.UK Prepare).
  • In England and Wales, Ofwat says that if the piped supply fails, companies must provide at least 10 litres per person per day within the first 24 hours, and keep this up until the supply is back. This may be bottled water, bowsers or standpipes.
  • Some companies say more. For example, Wessex Water says it will provide bottled water if your supply is off for longer than five hours.
  • Keep bottled water for drinking, washing hands, cooking and baby formula. GOV.UK says soft and fizzy drinks from the cupboard are safe to drink too.
  • Water from a water butt is fine for flushing the toilet, but do not drink it (GOV.UK Prepare, Thames Water).
  • Paper plates, wet wipes, hand sanitiser and no-wash shampoo help save water (Thames Water).
  • Check on neighbours who may struggle to collect water.

Babies and medical needs

  • Baby formula: the NHS says bottled water is not usually recommended for making up formula, because it is not sterile and may contain too much salt (sodium) or sulphate. In rare cases, such as when drinking water is not available, you can use it, but still follow the NHS step-by-step guide. GOV.UK Prepare says that if you cannot boil water, try to use ready-to-use formula.
  • Medical devices that need water, such as home dialysis: GOV.UK Prepare says to talk to your care team now about a plan for a water cut.

Get on your water company’s Priority Services Register

Water companies keep a free register of people who need extra help, separate from the energy one. Ofwat says people likely to benefit include those of pensionable age, disabled people, people with an illness that affects day-to-day life, people who are pregnant or live with children aged 5 and under, and people with sight, hearing or memory problems, among others. Help can include bottled water deliveries during a supply cut.

Sign up before you need it. Thames Water says applications can take time, and registering during an interruption does not mean help arrives straight away. See our Priority Services Register guide.

When the water comes back

GOV.UK Prepare says discoloured water is common afterwards. Run the cold tap nearest your stop tap (usually the kitchen sink) for a while. If it does not clear, contact your water company.
