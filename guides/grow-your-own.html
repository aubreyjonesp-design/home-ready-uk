Grow some of your own food

Last checked: 15 October 2026

Growing a little food is satisfying, cuts waste and can save some money. It adds to your stored food. It does not replace it.

Be realistic. Most crops take weeks or months to grow, and a garden gives most of its food in summer and autumn. Growing your own will not feed a household through a short emergency such as a power cut or a few days of bad weather. For that, keep a few days of food and water you already eat. Use our kit and water calculator.

Easy crops for small spaces

You do not need a garden. Pots, troughs and grow bags on a patio, balcony or sunny windowsill all work. The RHS suggests containers at least 45cm (18in) deep and wide where you can, otherwise you will need to water and feed more often.

  • Windowsill: herbs such as basil, parsley and chives, pea shoots and “cut and come again” salad leaves. Salad leaves can grow in containers only 10 to 15cm deep.
  • Balcony or patio pots: tomatoes, chillies, dwarf beans, radishes, beetroot, carrots in a deep pot, spinach, chard and lettuce.
  • Bigger pots or a small bed: potatoes in a large bag or tub, runner beans up a cane, courgettes.
  • Use peat-free compost and pots with drainage holes. Pots dry out quickly, so water often in summer. Collected rainwater is ideal for established plants (see our rainwater guide).
  • On a balcony, check weight limits and any rules in your lease or tenancy, and make sure pots cannot fall.

A simple UK growing year

Timings vary across the UK. The north and high ground are usually a few weeks later than the south. Always check the seed packet.

Rough guide for common easy crops
SeasonWhat you might do
Late winter (Feb to Mar)Plan, buy seed and compost. Sow chillies and tomatoes indoors on a warm windowsill. Chit seed potatoes.
Spring (Mar to May)Sow salad leaves, radishes, peas, beetroot and carrots. Plant potatoes. Sow beans and courgettes indoors in late spring.
Early summer (late May to Jun)Once frosts have passed, move tomatoes, beans and courgettes outside. Keep sowing salad every few weeks.
Summer (Jul to Aug)Main picking season. Water regularly. Sow more salad and some winter crops, such as chard.
Autumn (Sep to Nov)Lift potatoes and the last crops. Save seed. Store and preserve. Plant garlic.
Winter (Dec to Jan)Little grows. Pick hardy crops such as kale and leeks. Clean pots and plan for next year.

Storing and preserving the harvest

  • Gardens often give too much at once. Share it, or store it.
  • Freezing: Garden Organic says many vegetables, such as peas, beans, broccoli and leeks, freeze well if blanched in boiling water first. Tomatoes are best cooked into a sauce first. Follow the Food Standards Agency’s advice on chilling, freezing and defrosting food safely.
  • Cool, dark storage: some crops, such as potatoes and squash, keep for a while somewhere cool, dark and dry. Check them often and use any damaged ones first.
  • Jams, chutneys and pickles: use a trusted, tested recipe and clean jars. Do not guess with home bottling or canning of low-acid vegetables.
  • A freezer relies on power. In a power cut, keep it closed (see our power cut guide).

Allotments

An allotment gives you much more space at a low yearly rent. In England and Wales, apply through your local council. GOV.UK says the council will either offer you a plot or, in many cases, add your name to a waiting list. Some lists are long, so it is worth applying early. In Scotland and Northern Ireland, contact your local council. Allotment societies and the National Allotment Society can also help.

Saving your own seed

  • Easy seeds to start with include peas, beans, lettuce and tomatoes.
  • The RHS says to save seed only from healthy plants, collect it on a dry day when it is ripe, dry it well, then store it in labelled paper envelopes in an airtight container somewhere cool and dry.
  • Seed from F1 hybrid varieties (it says on the packet) may not grow true to the parent plant.
  • Garden Organic’s Heritage Seed Library shares older varieties with its members.

The costs

Growing can save some money, especially on herbs and salad, but it is not free. You will need seeds, compost, pots and perhaps tools, canes and netting, and water in dry spells. Allotments have a yearly rent set locally. Start small with a few things you like to eat, reuse pots and containers, and swap seeds and plants with neighbours. Prices vary a lot, so we do not give figures here.
