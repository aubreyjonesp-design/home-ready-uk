Storing food for emergencies

Last checked: 15 October 2026

A small store of food you already eat makes a power cut, water cut or snowed-in week much easier. Build it up slowly and use it, so nothing goes to waste.

GOV.UK Prepare does not give a set amount of food to keep. It says how much you need depends on your own circumstances. It suggests food that does not need cooking, such as ready-to-eat tinned meat, fruit or vegetables, plus a tin opener.

Keep a rotation store

  • Buy a few extra of the long-life foods you normally eat. Put new ones at the back and use the oldest first.
  • Check dates when you buy and when you put things away. The Food Standards Agency (FSA) says a use-by date is about safety, and a best before date is about quality. Food can be eaten after its best before date, but may not taste as good.
  • The British Red Cross suggests keeping 3 days’ supply of bottled water and long-life, ready-to-eat food, with a tin opener. Use our kit calculator for water amounts.

Tins and dried food

  • Tins are useful because, the FSA says, many tinned foods are safe to eat without heating. Check the label, as it depends on the product.
  • A shallow dent is usually fine. The FSA says do not eat food from a deeply dented, rusty or bulging can.
  • Once a tin is open, do not store food in it. Put leftovers in a bowl or container in the fridge.
  • Store dried and cupboard foods in a clean, dry place and follow any storage instructions on the pack.

Food that needs no cooking

Electric and induction hobs will not work in a power cut. The FSA says a camping stove or barbecue can be used, but most are designed for outdoor use only. Never use them indoors, because of the risk of carbon monoxide (see our first aid guide). Good no-cook options include ready-to-eat tins, crackers, cereal bars, nut butter and dried fruit, if your household can eat them.

Food safety in a power cut

From the FSA’s power cut advice:

  • A power cut of less than 4 hours is unlikely to affect chilled or frozen food.
  • Keep fridge and freezer doors closed. A fridge should stay cold for up to 4 hours. A full freezer should stay frozen for up to 48 hours (24 hours if half full). These are estimates and vary by appliance.
  • A fridge thermometer helps. In a power cut, food can be kept in a fridge below 8°C. If it reaches 8°C or above, throw away chilled food (such as food with a use-by date, “keep refrigerated” products and leftovers) after 4 hours, unless the power comes back within 4 hours.
  • If it is cold outside (below 8°C), you can store food outside in a clean, sealed box, out of the sun and away from animals.
  • Eat in this order: fridge food first, then defrosted food that needs no cooking, then perishable food kept at room temperature, then tins, jars and dried food.
  • Take extra care for young children, pregnant women, older people and anyone with a health condition.

Never use the “sniff test” for food with a use-by date. The FSA says you cannot smell the bugs that cause food poisoning. Read the full FSA power cut advice.

Special diets and allergies

  • Store foods that everyone in your home can safely eat, and keep them separate if someone has a food allergy or coeliac disease.
  • Check labels each time you buy. The FSA says food businesses must tell you if they use any of the 14 regulated allergens.
  • If someone uses an adrenaline auto-injector, the FSA says it is important to carry it with them.
  • Write any allergies on your household plan.

Babies

GOV.UK Prepare says ready-made (“ready-to-feed”) formula is best for emergencies, because you may not be able to boil water or sterilise bottles. The FSA says making up powdered formula safely needs boiled water. The NHS says ready-to-feed formula costs more and must be used more quickly once opened. Keep nappies and wipes too.

Pets

GOV.UK Prepare reminds you not to forget food for pets, and says you may need extra water for them. Keep a few days of your pet’s usual food and any medicines. Ask your vet if you are unsure what your pet needs.
