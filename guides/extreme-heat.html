Extreme heat: staying safe in hot weather

Last checked: 15 October 2026

Many people enjoy hot weather, but too much heat can make anyone unwell. Simple steps keep you, your home and the people around you safer.

Heatstroke is a medical emergency. Call 999 if someone has a very high temperature, hot skin that is not sweating, fast breathing or heartbeat, confusion, a fit or loses consciousness, or is still unwell after 30 minutes of cooling down.

Get ready before summer

Do these now, while things are calm. Each one only takes a few minutes or a small cost.

  • Find your coolest room. Note which rooms stay coolest on a warm day, usually away from the sun. UKHSA says to move to a cooler part of the house, especially for sleeping.
  • Fit or check blinds and curtains on sunny windows, especially south, east and west facing. UKHSA suggests internal blinds or curtains. CIBSE says reflective films on windows can also help.
  • Shade windows from the outside if you can. UKHSA says external shutters, roller blinds or awnings are very effective, and plants outside can shade south-facing windows. CIBSE says external shading works far better than inside blinds, and even a fabric screen fixed outside a window helps.
  • Check your fan works in spring, or buy one before demand rises. Remember UKHSA’s advice: use fans only when the air is below 35°C, and do not aim them straight at your body.
  • Get an indoor thermometer. The NHS says to check the temperature of rooms, especially where people at higher risk live and sleep.
  • Check how your medicines must be stored. UKHSA says some must be kept below 25°C or in the fridge. Ask your pharmacist if you are unsure, and keep taking them unless told otherwise.
  • Get a refillable water bottle for each person, and keep some sealed bottled water in case hot weather affects water supplies (see our no water guide).
  • Pack a summer kit: sunscreen of at least SPF 30 with 4 or 5 star UVA protection (UKHSA, citing the NHS), wide-brimmed hats, sunglasses, light loose clothes, and a spray bottle or cold packs for cooling skin.
  • Agree who you will check on, such as an older neighbour or relative, and how (a call or a visit each day in hot weather). Ask someone to check on you if you are at higher risk.
  • Sign up for warnings. Use the Met Office app to set alerts for your area, or subscribe to Met Office email alerts on the warnings page. In England, register for UKHSA Heat-Health Alerts.
  • Plan shade and water for pets. Dogs Trust says make sure your dog has shade and plenty of fresh water all day.
  • Fit a water butt before a dry spell. During a hosepipe ban, water companies such as Essex & Suffolk Water say you can still use collected rainwater. See our rainwater guide.
  • Plan travel and work around hot days. GOV.UK Prepare says allow extra time for journeys, as heat can damage roads and slow trains, and take extra water. Plan active jobs for early morning or evening.
  • Think longer term. If your home overheats (for example a top-floor flat or windows that barely open), GOV.UK Prepare says contact your local council, letting agency or Citizens Advice. The NHS says council environmental health teams can inspect a rented home for excess heat. If you are insulating or refurbishing, UKHSA says ask installers about reducing overheating. New homes in England must meet Building Regulations Part O (overheating).

Warnings and alerts

  • Met Office Extreme Heat warnings cover the whole UK. Like other Met Office warnings, they are based on the likely impact, not a set temperature. The Met Office says amber and red extreme heat warnings can be issued. Red means dangerous weather is expected, with a risk to life: act now. See Met Office UK warnings and the warnings guide.
  • Heat-Health Alerts come from the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) with the Met Office. They cover England only. The main season runs from 1 June to 30 September, with extra alerts outside it if needed. They are aimed at health and care services, but anyone can view or sign up for them:
    • Yellow: heat likely to affect mainly people who are particularly vulnerable, or a less certain forecast that could get worse.
    • Amber: impacts likely across the whole health service, with the whole population potentially at risk.
    • Red: significant risk to life, even for healthy people. It is issued with a red Extreme Heat warning.
    See the Heat-Health Alert service and current alerts.

Who is most at risk

UKHSA lists people aged 65 and over, babies and children aged 5 and under, and pregnant women. It also includes people with heart or breathing problems, dementia, diabetes, kidney disease, Parkinson’s disease or mobility problems, and people on certain medicines. Others at risk are people with serious mental health problems, people who are already ill and dehydrated, and people with alcohol or drug dependence. So are people who are active or work outside, people experiencing homelessness, and people who live alone.

Keeping your home cool (UKHSA “Beat the heat”)

  • Close blinds and curtains on windows in direct sun during the day. Close outside shutters if you have them.
  • Open windows, if it is safe, when the air outside feels cooler than inside, for example at night. Try to get air flowing through.
  • Use electric fans if the air temperature is below 35°C, but do not aim them directly at your body.
  • Check the heating is off, and turn off lights and equipment you are not using.
  • Move to a cooler room, especially for sleeping. Libraries, places of worship or supermarkets may be cooler than your home.
  • Stay out of the sun between 11am and 3pm, and plan activity for the early morning or evening.

Heat exhaustion or heatstroke?

Heat exhaustion (NHS): tiredness, dizziness, headache, feeling or being sick, heavy sweating with pale clammy skin, cramps, a high temperature, intense thirst and irritability. It usually does not need emergency help if the person cools down within 30 minutes:

  • Move them somewhere cool and remove unnecessary clothing.
  • Give plenty of water, or a sports or rehydration drink.
  • Spray or sponge their skin with cool water and fan them. Cold packs wrapped in cloth under the armpits or on the neck help too.
  • Stay with them. Call 111 if you are struggling to treat it or need advice.

Heatstroke is an emergency. Call 999 for the signs in the box at the top. While you wait, the NHS says to wrap them in a cool wet sheet, or fan or sponge them with cold water. If they become unresponsive, follow our first aid guide (recovery position or CPR). Read more: NHS: Heat exhaustion and heatstroke · St John Ambulance: Heatstroke.

Drinks and medicines

  • Drink fluids regularly. UKHSA says enough that your pee is a pale straw colour. Water, diluted squash and lower-fat milk are good choices. Limit alcohol, which dehydrates you.
  • Keep taking your prescribed medicines unless a health professional tells you otherwise. Some medicines must be stored below 25°C or in the fridge, so check the pack.
  • The NHS says people on several medicines, or medicines that may make heat worse, are at higher risk. Ask a pharmacist if you are unsure about your medicines in the heat, or call 111.

Checking on others, children, babies and pets

  • Check on family, friends and neighbours at higher risk, and ask them to check on you if you are at risk.
  • Never leave babies, children, older people or pets alone in a parked car or other small closed space. UKHSA says they can get dangerously hot very quickly.
  • Shade prams with a parasol, keep air flowing, remove extra clothing and check babies often. Children should not do hard exercise on very hot days.
  • Dogs Trust says winding a window down is not enough, and a dog could die in a hot car in minutes. Walk dogs at cooler times of day. If you see a dog showing signs of heatstroke in a hot car, the RSPCA says call 999.
  • If your dog overheats, the RSPCA says “cool first, transport second”: stop exercise, get it into shade, pour cool (not ice-cold) water over its body but not its head, then take it to a vet.

Swimming and cold water shock

Rivers, lakes and the sea are often much colder than they look, even in summer. The RNLI says water below 15°C can cause cold water shock, which affects your breathing and movement and can lead to drowning. It says average UK and Ireland sea temperatures are just 12°C.

  • If you fall in, the RNLI says: take a minute, as the first effects pass in less than a minute. Relax and float, then call for help or swim to safety if you can.
  • UKHSA advises swimming only where there are lifeguards, never after alcohol, always with someone else, and getting out as soon as you feel cold.
  • Wear a buoyancy aid or life jacket for boating, fishing or activities at the water’s edge.

Wildfires and barbecues

  • The National Fire Chiefs Council (NFCC) says only have barbecues and campfires in designated areas, never leave them unattended, and put them out fully with water.
  • Keep a barbecue on a flat surface that cannot burn, well away from fences, sheds and plants, with water nearby. Never use one indoors or on a balcony, and never use petrol or other flammable liquids to light it.
  • Take litter home. Glass bottles can focus the sun and start a fire. Put cigarettes out properly.
  • Follow any local restrictions and the advice of the emergency services. GOV.UK has current guidance saying disposable barbecues should not be sold during the present drought and heatwave conditions in Great Britain.
  • If you see a wildfire, the NFCC says move to a place of safety and call 999. Do not assume someone else has reported it.

Hosepipe bans and saving water

Ofwat says water companies can bring in a hosepipe ban (a “Temporary Use Ban”) when supplies are low after long dry spells. There is still enough water to drink and wash. You cannot use a hosepipe for things like watering the garden, filling a paddling pool or washing the car, but you can use a watering can or bucket. Ofwat says breaking the rules can lead to a fine of up to £1,000. Check your water company’s website for local rules and exemptions. Collected rainwater is ideal for the garden: see our rainwater guide.

Power cuts in hot weather

UKHSA says to look out for advice if power or water supplies are likely to be affected. Fans and fridges stop working in a power cut, so keep a battery or wind-up torch and radio and a charged power bank, and keep fridge and freezer doors closed. Call 105 to report a power cut. See our power cut guide and food storage guide.
