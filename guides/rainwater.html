Collecting rainwater safely

Last checked: 15 October 2026

A water butt saves mains water and is handy for the garden and for flushing the toilet if your water goes off. But it is not drinking water.

Do not drink untreated rainwater, or use it for cooking, brushing teeth or making baby formula. Rain picks up bird and animal droppings, dirt and pollution from roofs and gutters. In a water outage, use bottled water or water from your water company. See our no water guide.

Get ready now

Do these now, while things are calm. Each one only takes a few minutes or a small cost.

  • If you rent or own a leasehold flat, check your tenancy or lease, or ask your landlord, before fitting a water butt.
  • Fit a water butt to a downpipe on a firm, level stand, with a leaf filter and a close-fitting lid (RHS, UKHSA).
  • Make sure children cannot climb in or open the lid.
  • Label it “not drinking water” and keep bottled water for drinking (see our no water guide).
  • Never connect it to your mains pipes (Water Regs UK).
  • Fit it before a dry spell. During a hosepipe ban, Essex & Suffolk Water says you can still use collected rainwater.

Water butts and sizes

  • A water butt connects to a gutter downpipe on your house, garage, shed or greenhouse. The RHS calls a 240-litre butt “standard”. Smaller and slimline butts are available for tight spaces, and you can link several together.
  • Even a small roof collects a lot. The RHS says the roof of a small shed in dry south-east England can collect 2,500 to 3,500 litres a year.
  • Stand the butt on a firm, level stand so a watering can fits under the tap. Fit a leaf filter and a close-fitting lid.

Good uses for rainwater

  • The garden: established plants, pots and houseplants. The RHS advises not using it on seedlings, as it may carry diseases that harm them.
  • Flushing the toilet in a water outage: pour a bucket into the bowl or the cistern.
  • Outdoor cleaning, such as washing the car, bikes or tools.
  • Avoid spraying stored water as a fine mist. The NHS says Legionnaires’ disease is caught by breathing in tiny water droplets containing the bacteria, and the RHS advises not to use stored water for misting or sprinklers if it is above 20°C.

What filtering and boiling do, and don’t, do

  • Leaf filters and screens keep out leaves and debris. They do not make water safe to drink.
  • Boiling kills or inactivates germs. The Drinking Water Inspectorate (DWI) says bringing water to a rolling boil kills bacteria and viruses and is effective against parasites such as Cryptosporidium.
  • Boiling does not remove chemicals, dirt or pollution. Water companies issue a “do not drink” notice, rather than a “boil water” notice, when water may contain things that boiling cannot remove.
  • Not all home water filters can be relied on to remove germs. Rainwater for drinking needs proper treatment, testing and upkeep, to the standards that apply to private water supplies. That is a job for specialists, not a household water butt.
  • If you ever need to boil tap water, follow the DWI advice on boil water notices.

Is it legal?

Yes. The RHS says collecting rainwater from roofs is perfectly legal in the UK. If you rent, or own a leasehold flat, check your tenancy agreement or lease, or ask your landlord or managing agent, before fitting a butt or changing gutters and downpipes.

Keep it clean: algae and mosquitoes

  • Keep a close-fitting lid on. The RHS says this keeps out plant disease spores and discourages algae. UKHSA advises a well-fitted lid on water butts to stop mosquitoes breeding.
  • Keep gutters clear and use the water regularly so it does not sit for long.
  • Clean the butt once a year with warm water and a little washing-up liquid, and empty and clean it if it smells.
  • Empty or turn over buckets and other containers that collect rain.
  • Place butts out of direct sun if you can, to keep the water cooler.
  • Make sure young children cannot climb in or open the lid.

Keep it separate from your mains water

  • Never connect a water butt or rainwater pipe to your mains water pipes, even with a hose. Dirty water can flow backwards (backflow) and contaminate drinking water in your home and your neighbours’ homes.
  • Water Regs UK says other water sources such as rainwater must never be connected directly to mains drinking water, and you should tell your water company if your home has both. Mains back-up needs a proper air gap.
  • Whole-house rainwater systems that supply toilets or washing machines must meet the Water Supply (Water Fittings) Regulations 1999. Use an approved plumber and keep pipes labelled.
